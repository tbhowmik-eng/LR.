Operating Speed & Crash Outcomes – Literature Summary Dashboard

This dashboard offers a clear and organized look at what past research tells us about operating speed and roadway safety. It brings together key studies, highlights the speed measures researchers commonly use, and shows how these measures relate to crash outcomes across different road types. You can explore individual studies interactively or scroll down to see overall patterns and insights. The goal is to make the existing evidence easy to understand, compare, and apply; whether you are reviewing methods, identifying trends, or looking for gaps that future research can address.

Summary: U.S.-based work is dominated by probe-based space-mean datasets such as NPMRDS and HERE. 7. Integrated Summary of Key Findings on Speed–Crash Relationships

A. Mean Operating Speed Effects

  • Mean speed shows mixed relationships with crashes.
  • Rural and freeway studies sometimes show lower crash counts at higher mean speeds, reflecting better design and more homogeneous flow.
  • Urban and arterial environments consistently show higher crash frequency as mean speed increases.
  • Mean speed alone is not a reliable explanatory variable without contextual controls.

B. Speed Variability as the Most Consistent Predictor

  • Across 8 studies, higher speed variability (SD, CoV, lane differences, hourly/monthly variation) is associated with higher crash frequency in nearly all contexts.
  • Variability captures turbulence in flow, inconsistent driver behavior, and spatial/temporal heterogeneity in speeds.
  • It is a strong candidate for inclusion in next-generation HSM models where data permit.

C. Distribution-Based Metrics (85th Percentile, Differentials)

  • Higher 85th percentile speeds are generally linked to higher crash rates where measured.
  • Speed differentials relative to posted limits show that larger gaps (excess speeding or unusually low speeds) are associated with higher crash frequencies.

D. Combined Effects of High Speed and High Variability

  • High mean speeds and high variability each elevate crash risk on their own.
  • When they occur together, risk increases nonlinearly and more sharply than with either factor alone.
  • This supports using at least two speed descriptors (level + variability) in predictive models.

E. Temporal Speed Measures

  • Higher weekend–weekday differences and larger temporal variability in speed are associated with more crashes.
  • Speed stability over time appears as important as average level.

F. Data Quality and Aggregation Effects

  • Aggregated space-mean datasets (e.g., NPMRDS averages) can yield weaker or counterintuitive relationships.
  • Vehicle-level or high-frequency time-mean data produce more consistent and interpretable results.
  • This supports the need for a multi-tier operating-speed framework linking detailed trajectory data with broad space-mean archives.

8. High-Level Synthesis

  1. Speed variability is the strongest and most reliable predictor of crashes, outperforming mean and percentile speeds in most settings.
  2. Mean speed effects depend heavily on roadway context and design quality, explaining some counterintuitive findings.
  3. Distribution-based metrics (85th percentile, within/between-lane variation, CoV) provide stronger predictive power when available.
  4. Aggregated space-mean data can mask true behavioral relationships, leading to misleading conclusions.
  5. Urban streets and arterials show consistently positive speed–crash relationships for both speed level and variability.
  6. Freeway studies reveal nonlinear trends, including plateauing crash rates at high speeds and strong effects near flow breakdown.
  7. Severity-specific analyses consistently find larger impacts of speed on serious and fatal crashes than on minor outcomes.
